01 EMU Rotation & Reading Workflow▸

Daily Flow

  • Come in at 8am. Scrubs are fine.
  • Talk to Amanda/EMU team across the EMU reading room — ask for overnight events and all admissions for the day. Get the new admission's name, DOB, and arrival time.
  • Start reading EEGs on your own before rounds.
  • Write Progress Notes on EMU patients — use the "Medicine Progress Note" template, just rename it EMU Progress Note.
  • Discuss priority reads first. Communicate findings with the Gen Neuro/Jewish/Stroke team.
  • Most attendings will have you write the notes — save their dot phrases for their preferred format.
  • Admit patients until 12pm if half-day, until 4pm if whole-day.
  • Check out important items to the Jewish Team, including impending consults. It helps to start the H&P (via Epic notes) for pending admissions and give the Jewish team a heads-up.

What the Attending Will Ask

  • A three-liner: reason for admission/consult
  • History of seizures or semiology if possible
  • Current ASMs and sedatives
  • Whether there's any brain imaging
  • What you see on the EEG — mark anything you're curious about
Pearl Sample three-liner: "Mr. Apple is a 55M with known epilepsy, found down, witnessed to have myoclonic movements of his left arm. He's currently on Keppra and Vimpat. CTH showed encephalomalacia in the left occipital lobe. On my review, I saw focal slowing in the left parietal region." (Extend this as your comfort with reading grows.)

How to Admit an EMU Patient

  • Once the patient is in the building, put in Admission (Bed Slip) orders: Admit to In Patient > Med Surge > Reason: Convulsion
  • Enter the EMU Admission Orders (Order Set) — confirm Diet, EEG, Ativan/Diazepam, CBC, CMP, HCG (if female), DVT PPX
  • Chart-check the patient even before they arrive in the building
  • See the patient and ask the admit questions below — no need for an extensive HPI, since they likely already have one in Epic from the referring neurologist
  • Check for any devices (VNS or RNS) that need to be interrogated or turned off
  • After seeing the patient, call the EMU attending to discuss — which ASMs to continue or stop
  • Do the med rec and resume necessary medications
  • Post information on SharePoint and check out to the Jewish Team

What to Ask EMU Admits

  • Referring physician
  • Reason for EMU stay
  • Last episode
  • Frequency of this episode
  • Last dose of their ASM
02 Basic Physiology▸
  • Resting membrane potential (~ −70 mV): the inside of the neuron is negatively charged relative to the outside.
  • Ion movement at rest: K⁺ moves in, Na⁺ moves out via the Na⁺/K⁺ pump.
  • Action potential: increased Na⁺ permeability triggers a rapid Na⁺ influx.
  • Depolarization: Na⁺ channels open, allowing Na⁺ entry until they close.
  • Voltage-dependent channels open and close based on membrane potential.
  • Time-dependent closure: Na⁺ channels close automatically after activation.
  • Signal propagation: depolarization spreads along the membrane via electrotonic conduction.
  • Repolarization: K⁺ exits, restoring the resting potential.
  • Hyperpolarization: excess K⁺ efflux may briefly make the membrane more negative than −70 mV.
  • Excitatory signals (EPSPs): neurotransmitters like ACh and glutamate open Na⁺/Ca²⁺ channels, increasing neuron activity.
  • Inhibitory signals (IPSPs): GABA opens K⁺/Cl⁻ channels, decreasing neuron activity.

Action potential: Na⁺ influx drives depolarization; K⁺ efflux drives repolarization and transient hyperpolarization.

Pearl EEG measures the difference between EPSPs and IPSPs in the extracellular matrix, reflecting the summed electrical activity of cortical neurons.
Pearl Approximately 6 cm² of cortical surface must be synchronously activated for a discharge to be visible on scalp EEG.
03 Equipment & the 10-20 System▸
  • Electrodes are where electrocortical potentials are conducted and amplified.
  • Each EEG channel displays the potential difference between two electrodes.
  • Electrodes are placed using the 10-20 system — the skull is divided into 10% or 20% increments for electrode placement, keeping positioning consistent across different head shapes and sizes.

04 Setting Up in Natus▸

Step 1 — Log In

Log in to uapps.uoflhealth.org. Note: uapps.ulh.org will NOT have Natus.

Step 2 — Open Natus

Click the Natus Database icon. If too many Natus sessions are open at once, yours may not load — text the group to close Natus unless someone else needs it.

Step 3 — Find the Study

The default view shows "Today's Studies." Double-click a name to open it.

Pearl Some attendings write their prelim results under "Study Name" — check there first.

Step 4 — Confirm Your Settings

Make sure these 5 settings are in place before you start reading:

LFF (Low Frequency Filter)Filters out low-frequency activity
HFF (High Frequency Filter)Filters out high-frequency activity
Notch FilterFilters a specific frequency — 60 Hz in North America (outlet electrical fields), 50 Hz elsewhere
SensitivityRatio of input voltage to output, in µV/mm. Lower sensitivity = smaller voltage displayed
Time BaseHorizontal time scale. Usual is 30 mm/sec (30 mm = 1 second). Faster = better for rapid changes; slower = better for slow rhythms

Try different filter settings and see how the tracing changes.

Step 5 — Calibrate

Edit > Calibrate > Monitor Display, or change Time Base to 15 mm/sec. You should see 10 seconds per page — dark green gridline to dark green gridline is one second; light green to light green is 0.2 seconds.

Calibration: dark-green-to-dark-green = 1 second; light-green-to-light-green = 0.2 seconds.

Step 6 — Scroll Mode

Change "Page" to "Scroll" and press spacebar to watch the EEG move in real time. Adjust playback "Speed" as needed.

Step 7 — Review Video

Click the camera icon to watch the patient video for an event. Video can only run 1–2 seconds before distortion and lag set in — don't push it faster than that.

Step 8 — Annotate

Press spacebar to pause. Align the red-line marker to the area of interest, type your annotation, and click OK.

As a resident, avoid marking "SEIZURE" or "SZ" — use descriptive text instead. Labeling an event as a seizure in the record can lead others to misinterpret and treat unnecessarily before it's confirmed.

Align the red-line marker to the event before typing the annotation.

Step 9 — Save

You have to save (Ctrl+S) your annotation for anyone else to see it. When you exit Natus, a "Study Information" box pops up — avoid writing in the big white box, since some attendings use it for their preliminary read. Just click OK, then Yes to save changes.

Troubleshooting — Lead Artifact

If you see a messy "green line" contaminating multiple channels, it's likely the ECG lead artifact. Right-click the ECG lead on the left and select "Hide Selected." You can do this for any loose lead — but always tell the EEG tech so they can fix it.

Loose-lead artifact: right-click the offending channel → Hide Selected, and notify the EEG tech.

06 Amplitude▸

Amplitude is the measure of EEG voltage change — the height of a waveform, measured in µV. In practice, it's a proxy for "voltage." Normal adult amplitude ranges from 10–100 µV.

07 Frequency▸

Frequency describes the number of complete cycles/repetitive waves in one second, measured in Hertz (Hz).

Mnemonic: D-T-A-B → 4 – 8 – 12 – 30
BandRangeSeen In
Delta0–4 HzSlowest frequency. Should not be present in the normal awake brain. Seen in sleep, structural abnormalities, or encephalopathy.
Theta4–8 HzSeen in drowsiness and in children. Most likely abnormal if it dominates the PDR in a waking adult, or if focal. Hallmark of some normal findings such as Rhythmic Theta of Drowsiness.
Alpha8–12 HzSeen in normal awake patients over the posterior region; best seen with eyes closed. AKA the Posterior Dominant Rhythm (PDR).
Beta13–30 HzSeen with benzodiazepines, barbiturates, anesthetics. Also seen in myogenic artifact.
Gamma> 30 Hz—

Trace and Count

To determine frequency by eye: trace the wave and count how many complete cycles occur in 1 second (use the gridlines from calibration).

Worked examples — bracket one second of tracing and count the cycles inside it (both examples: 8 Hz).

08 Background Activity & PDR▸

Posterior Dominant "Alpha" Rhythm (PDR)

  • Rhythmic activity seen at the occipital or parietal regions
  • Predominantly present during wakefulness while the eyes are closed
  • Usually in the alpha frequency band in a healthy adult
  • Wait > 1 second after eye closure before determining the frequency

Posterior dominant alpha rhythm, ~8 Hz, emerging after eye closure.

09 Montage▸

An EEG montage is the map or pattern used to connect scalp electrodes to the EEG machine — it defines which electrodes are compared to which to record brain wave activity.

1. Bipolar Montage

  • Each channel compares two neighboring electrodes (e.g., Fp1–F3, F3–C3)
  • Useful for localizing abnormalities via phase reversals — a phase reversal occurs when two adjacent electrodes pick up electrical activity that is out of sync, so the waveforms deflect in opposite directions (one up, one down)
  • Example: Double banana — a common bipolar setup that curves like bananas over each hemisphere

2. Referential Montage

  • Each electrode is compared to a common reference (e.g., A1 or Cz)
  • Good for amplitude comparison
  • Easier to spot generalized vs. focal activity

Double banana bipolar montage — chains curve over each hemisphere from frontopolar to occipital.

10 Morphology▸

Morphology refers to the shape and physical characteristics of the EEG waves — described as monomorphic or polymorphic.

Monomorphic: waves are about the same frequency and amplitude throughout.

GPDs with triphasic morphology — sit on the ictal–interictal continuum.

Polymorphic delta activity — variable frequency and amplitude within the delta band.

11 Symmetry▸

Symmetry describes approximately equal amplitude, frequency, and form of EEG activity over homologous areas on opposite sides of the head.

  • Right hemisphere = red lines = even electrode numbers
  • Left hemisphere = blue lines = odd electrode numbers
  • Compare Fp2-F8 (right) with Fp1-F7 (left); compare Fp2-F4 (right) with Fp1-F3 (left)

13 Timing▸

Timing describes the temporal relationship of discharges across different regions.

  • Synchronous: discharges occurring at two regions simultaneously
  • Asynchronous: discharges occur independently of one another

Synchronous — the discharge appears in the left and right frontal regions at the same time.

14 Rhythmicity▸

Rhythmicity describes recurring waves of equal duration.

  • Regular: uniform — individual waves are consistent in shape and duration
  • Irregular: not uniform, but still has some underlying rhythm that can vary over time
  • Arrhythmic: waves of unequal duration and shape — no consistent pattern at all

Regular (top) vs. irregular (middle) vs. arrhythmic (bottom) patterns.

15 Sleep EEG▸

Stage 1

Drowsiness — fewer eye blinks, slow eye-roving movements — with loss of the PDR. Marked by POSTS and Vertex Waves.

  • POSTS (Positive Occipital Sharp Transients of Sleep): sharp, transient waves maximal at the occipital regions, single or repetitive, generally < 50 µV
  • Vertex Waves: sharply contoured waves maximal at the vertex, can occur in response to sensory (e.g., auditory) stimulus, rarely > 250 µV
Pearl Focal spikes or sharp waves are more often seen during Stage 1 and Stage 2 of sleep — don't miss them by assuming sleep tracings are "quiet."

Stage 1 — short run of vertex waves.

Stage 1 — large vertex waves.

Stage 1 — vertex waves.

Stage 2

Sleep marked by K Complexes and Sleep Spindles, with loss of the PDR.

  • K Complex: a negative sharp wave, often associated with a sleep spindle
  • Sleep Spindle: 11–16 Hz activity lasting > 0.5 s, maximal at the central regions

Stage 2 — sleep spindles.

Stage 2 — K complex with sleep spindles.

Stage 3 (Deep Sleep) & Stage 4

  • Stage 3: more delta (20–50% of the recording)
  • Stage 4: delta-predominant (> 50% of the recording); vertex waves and spindles are often absent

16 Normal EEG Variants▸

Mu Rhythm

  • Seen in the waking state
  • About 8–10 Hz
  • Occurs over the central or centroparietal regions
  • Attenuated by thought or actual movement, especially of the contralateral limb

Mu rhythm, ~8–10 Hz, central/centroparietal.

Mu rhythm in average montage.

Wicket Spikes

  • Seen in drowsiness and light sleep
  • More common in older patients
  • Spike-like monophasic surface-negative single waves or trains of waves over the temporal regions
  • Can be seen in patients with epilepsy — don't over-call these as epileptiform

17 Artifacts▸

Chewing

Periodic high-amplitude activity in the frontotemporal regions that correlates with the patient on video. Note the lack of evolution — a key differentiator from a true seizure pattern.

Chewing artifact — periodic, high-amplitude, frontotemporal, no evolution.

Looking Left / Eye Movement

Eye movement produces characteristic deflections in the frontal leads (the eye acts as a dipole) — recognize this pattern so it isn't mistaken for frontal slowing.

Eye-movement artifact from looking left.

Pulse Artifact

Rhythmic artifact time-locked to the cardiac cycle, usually from an electrode positioned near a pulsating vessel.

Pulse artifact — time-locked to the ECG trace below.

19 Epileptiform Discharges▸

Spikes and Sharps

  • Spike: transient, pointed peak, 20–70 ms
  • Sharp: transient, pointed peak, 70–200 ms
  • Polyspike: two or more spikes in a row, lasting < 0.5 seconds. If > 0.5 seconds, it qualifies as a BIRD (Brief Potentially Ictal Rhythmic Discharge)

Prevalence

Abundant> 1 per 10 seconds, but not periodic
Frequent> 1 per minute but < 1 per 10 seconds
Occasional> 1 per hour but < 1 per minute
Rare< 1 per hour
Pearl Memorize the prevalence cutoffs — they show up constantly in reports and rounding language.

Generalized spike-and-wave discharges.

Burst Suppression Pattern

  • Alternating bursts of theta and/or delta waves (often with sharp or fast activity) and periods of suppression (amplitude < 10 µV)
  • Suppression phases occupy more than 50% of the recording
  • Indicates severe brain dysfunction, or may be seen with certain anesthetic agents at deep sedation
  • Burst suppression with identical bursts following anoxic brain injury is associated with a poor neurological prognosis

Burst suppression pattern.

Triphasic Waves

High-amplitude (> 70 µV) positive sharp transients with preceding and following lower-amplitude negative waves. The first negative deflection is usually smaller and steeper than the one that follows the positive wave.

  • Bilateral, showing an antero-posterior or postero-anterior phase lag
  • Occur at ~1–2 Hz
  • Seen in runs, often with low-amplitude or absent background activity (< 40 µV) in between
  • Can change with arousal or noxious stimuli (regress or increase)
  • Most often seen in comatose patients with metabolic encephalopathy
  • Tend to reduce with sleep or IV benzodiazepines
20 Seizure & Status Epilepticus▸

Electrographic Seizure

Epileptiform discharges averaging > 2.5 Hz for > 10 seconds (> 25 discharges in 10 seconds), OR any pattern with definite evolution lasting > 10 seconds.

Electroclinical Seizure

A definite clinical correlate (time-locked) to the EEG pattern, of any duration — EEG and clinical improvement with an ASM.

Electroclinical Status Epilepticus

  • Electroclinical seizure for > 10 continuous minutes, OR > 20% of any 60-minute period
  • Ongoing electrographic seizures with bilateral tonic-clonic activity > 5 minutes

Ictal-Interictal Continuum (IIC)

Synonymous with "possible electrographic seizure" but is not itself a diagnosis — doesn't meet seizure criteria, but there is a reasonable chance it's contributing to impaired alertness.

Pearl Recommended reading: Understanding and Managing the Ictal-Interictal Continuum in Neurocritical Care by Sivaraju et al. Discuss borderline IIC cases with the Epilepsy attending.

21 Pearls & Quick Reference▸

Memorize

  • EEG measures the difference between EPSPs and IPSPs in the extracellular matrix — the summed activity of cortical neurons.
  • ~6 cm² of cortical surface must be synchronously activated to generate a visible discharge.
  • Frequency bands (D-T-A-B): Delta 0–4, Theta 4–8, Alpha 8–12, Beta 13–30, Gamma > 30 Hz.
  • Amplitude categories: Very Low < 20 µV, Low 20–49, Medium 50–149, High ≥ 150 µV.
  • Wait > 1 second after eye closure before reading PDR frequency.
  • Symmetry: mild asymmetry < 50% voltage or 0.5–1 Hz; marked asymmetry ≥ 50% voltage or > 1 Hz.
  • Prevalence: Abundant > 1/10sec, Frequent > 1/min, Occasional > 1/hr, Rare < 1/hr.
  • Electrographic seizure: > 2.5 Hz for > 10 sec, OR definite evolution > 10 sec.
  • Never annotate "SEIZURE" or "SZ" as a resident — describe findings instead.
  • Always save (Ctrl+S) annotations, and never write in the white box on exit.
