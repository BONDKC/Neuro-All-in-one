01 Why Did They Have a Stroke?▸

Determining stroke mechanism isn't academic — it directly guides:

  • Secondary prevention (antiplatelet vs. anticoagulation vs. etiology-specific therapy)
  • Prognosis
  • Risk of recurrence
  • What additional diagnostic workup is actually worth pursuing
Pearl If you don't know WHY the stroke happened, you don't know how to prevent the next one.
02 TOAST Classification▸

🟠 Large Artery Atherosclerosis

🧠 THINK: "Plaque"
Definition

≥50% stenosis (or <50% with other supporting evidence) of a major extracranial or intracranial artery supplying the infarct territory — a large-vessel definition covers the major named arteries (ICA, MCA, vertebrobasilar system); medium-vessel disease involves their proximal branches.

Typical Patient

Older, with vascular risk factors — hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, smoking history.

Typical Imaging Pattern

Cortical, territorial infarct. Watershed/borderzone pattern if the mechanism is hypoperfusion rather than embolism.

Common Causes
  • Atherosclerotic plaque, especially if ulcerated — a source for artery-to-artery embolism
  • Symptomatic carotid stenosis
Diagnostic Workup
  • CTA
  • MRA
  • Carotid ultrasound
  • DSA — reserved for equivocal noninvasive imaging
Long-Term Treatment

Antiplatelet therapy, high-intensity statin, aggressive risk factor control. Consider carotid endarterectomy or stenting for severe symptomatic stenosis (≥70%).

Pearl Artery-to-artery embolism from an ulcerated plaque can look identical to a cardioembolic infarct on imaging — the vessel imaging is what separates them.
Don't assume large-artery atherosclerosis just because plaque is present — the stenosis severity and location need to actually correlate with the infarct territory.

❤️ Cardioembolic

🧠 THINK: "Heart"
Definition

Stroke from a cardiac or aortic arch source of embolism.

Typical Patient

Known atrial fibrillation, recent MI, valve disease, reduced ejection fraction, or endocarditis risk factors.

Typical Imaging Pattern

Multiple vascular territories, cortical involvement, often larger infarcts than lacunar strokes.

Common Causes
  • Atrial fibrillation (most common)
  • Intracardiac thrombus
  • Mechanical valve
  • Endocarditis
  • Heart failure / reduced EF
Diagnostic Workup
  • ECG
  • Telemetry
  • TTE
  • TEE — higher sensitivity for LAA thrombus and valve vegetations
  • Loop recorder if suspicion remains high without an initial source found
Long-Term Treatment

Anticoagulation.

Pearl Think cardioembolic first when you see multiple vascular territories or simultaneous bilateral infarcts.
Don't anticoagulate large infarcts too early — hemorrhagic transformation risk. Timing depends on infarct size and clinical picture; discuss with the attending.

🫼 Small Vessel Disease

🧠 THINK: "Lacune"
Definition

Small subcortical infarct from occlusion of a single penetrating arteriole.

Typical Patient

Longstanding hypertension, diabetes.

Typical Imaging Pattern

Small subcortical or lacunar infarct — basal ganglia, internal capsule, thalamus, pons. Often multiple old lacunes and chronic microvascular changes on MRI.

Common Causes
  • Lipohyalinosis
  • Hypertension
  • Diabetes
  • Hyperlipidemia

Classic syndromes: pure motor hemiparesis, pure sensory stroke, ataxic hemiparesis, dysarthria-clumsy hand.

Diagnostic Workup

Often minimal beyond confirming the classic clinical/imaging picture — risk factor screening (HbA1c, lipid panel), consistent BP monitoring.

Long-Term Treatment

Antiplatelet therapy plus aggressive risk factor control.

Pearl No cortical signs (no aphasia, neglect, or visual field cut) — if present, reconsider the diagnosis.
Small subcortical infarcts can also be embolic (branch atheromatous disease) — don't default to lipohyalinosis without considering an embolic source, especially at a proximal MCA or basilar branch takeoff.

🧩 Other Determined Cause

🧠 THINK: "Rare"
Definition

Stroke with an identified non-atherosclerotic, non-cardioembolic, non-lacunar mechanism.

Typical Patient

Often younger (<50), with an atypical vascular risk factor profile.

Common Causes
  • Dissection
  • Hypoperfusion
  • Drug-induced vasospasm
  • Vasculitis
  • Moyamoya
  • Fibromuscular dysplasia
  • CADASIL
  • Fabry disease
  • MELAS
  • Antiphospholipid syndrome
  • Cancer-associated hypercoagulability
  • Pregnancy
  • Fat embolism
  • Air embolism
  • Tumor embolism
  • Paradoxical embolism / PFO
  • Pulmonary AVM
  • Iatrogenic causes
Diagnostic Workup

Tailored to the suspected etiology — vessel wall MRI for dissection/vasculitis, genetic testing for CADASIL, autoimmune/vasculitis panel, hypercoagulable panel, bubble study for PFO.

Long-Term Treatment

Etiology-specific.

Pearl Always consider this category in young stroke (<50) without typical vascular risk factors.
Don't stop the workup after finding one abnormality — multiple mechanisms can coexist, especially in young stroke.

🔍 Cryptogenic / ESUS

🧠 THINK: "Keep Looking"
Definition

ESUS (embolic stroke of undetermined source): a non-lacunar infarct without ≥50% proximal arterial stenosis, no major-risk cardioembolic source, and no other specific cause identified — after a complete workup.

Common Pitfall in Labeling

Incomplete workup is the most common reason a stroke gets mislabeled "cryptogenic." Before accepting the label, confirm the workup actually included: prolonged cardiac monitoring, TEE, cancer screening where indicated, and hypercoagulable testing in appropriate patients.

Diagnostic Workup
  • Extended cardiac monitoring (loop recorder) for occult paroxysmal AF
  • TEE for PFO, thrombus, or aortic arch atheroma
  • Cancer screening — especially with elevated D-dimer or multiple-territory infarcts without a clear source
  • Hypercoagulable panel in appropriate patients
Long-Term Treatment

Aspirin. Large trials of empiric anticoagulation in ESUS (NAVIGATE ESUS, RE-SPECT ESUS) did not show benefit over aspirin and increased bleeding risk — keep looking for a source rather than empirically anticoagulating.

Pearl "Cryptogenic" should be a diagnosis of exclusion after a complete workup, not a default label.
ESUS does NOT automatically require anticoagulation, even though it seems embolic by definition — the trial evidence doesn't support it.
03 Stroke Pattern Recognition▸
🧠 Multiple vascular territories↓❤️ Think cardioembolism
🧠 Pure motor stroke↓🫼 Think lacune
🧠 Young patient + neck pain↓🧩 Think dissection
🧠 Watershed infarcts↓🦄 Think hypoperfusion
🧠 Cortical infarct + carotid plaque↓🟠 Think large artery disease
🧠 Bilateral infarcts↓❤️ Look for embolic source
04 Stroke Risk Factors▸

Traditional Vascular

  • Hypertension
  • Diabetes
  • Hyperlipidemia
  • Smoking
  • Obesity

Cardiac

  • Atrial fibrillation
  • Heart failure / reduced EF
  • Valve disease
  • Recent MI
  • PFO

Lifestyle

  • Sedentary lifestyle
  • Alcohol use
  • Illicit drug use (cocaine, sympathomimetics)
  • Poor diet

Hypercoagulable

  • Antiphospholipid syndrome
  • Factor V Leiden
  • Protein C/S deficiency
  • Pregnancy/postpartum state

Cancer

  • Active malignancy (hypercoagulable state)
  • Nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis

Medication-Related

  • Oral contraceptives / hormone therapy
  • Anticoagulant non-adherence in known AF

Cancer Workup

  • CT Chest
  • CT Abdomen
  • CT Pelvis
  • D-dimer

When to order: multiple-territory infarcts without a clear cardioembolic or large-artery source, especially with an elevated D-dimer or otherwise unexplained hypercoagulable picture.

Hypercoagulable Workup

Who Should Receive Testing

Young patients, cryptogenic stroke after complete workup, or a personal/family history suggestive of a hypercoagulable state.

When to Order

After the more common etiologies (large vessel, cardioembolic, small vessel) have been reasonably excluded.

Typical Laboratory Evaluation

Antiphospholipid antibodies, protein C/S, factor V Leiden, and other thrombophilia markers as clinically indicated.

Pearl Hypercoagulable testing in the acute setting can be falsely abnormal — some values are best rechecked outpatient, away from the acute-phase reaction.

09 Don't Miss▸
Don't call every embolic-appearing stroke cryptogenic before the workup is actually complete.
Multiple vascular territories usually means cardioembolism until proven otherwise.
Young stroke requires evaluation for dissection, hypercoagulability, vasculitis, and genetic vasculopathies.
ESUS does NOT automatically require anticoagulation.
Always match secondary prevention to the actual stroke mechanism — not a default regimen.
