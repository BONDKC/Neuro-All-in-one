02 Mental Status▸

Level of Consciousness

Purpose

Establish baseline arousal before testing anything else.

Technique

Observe spontaneous arousal; escalate to verbal, then tactile/painful stimulus if needed.

Abnormal Findings

Lethargy, obtundation, stupor, coma.

Localization

Bilateral cortical dysfunction, or brainstem reticular activating system.

Orientation

Purpose

Screen for confusion/delirium.

Technique

Person, place, time, situation.

Abnormal Findings

Disorientation to time is usually first to go; disorientation to person is a late/severe finding.

Localization

Diffuse cortical dysfunction — not focally localizing.

Attention

Purpose

A prerequisite for every other cognitive domain — if attention is impaired, everything downstream is unreliable.

Technique

Digit span forward/backward, days of the week backward, spell "WORLD" backward.

Abnormal Findings

Poor digit span, can't sustain a task.

Localization

Frontal-subcortical networks; also impaired in delirium of any cause.

Memory

Purpose

Assess registration, short-term recall, and remote memory separately.

Technique

3-word registration and delayed recall; remote/autobiographical memory questions.

Abnormal Findings

Poor delayed recall with intact registration suggests a true memory disorder (e.g., hippocampal); poor registration suggests an attention problem instead.

Localization

Medial temporal lobe / hippocampus (episodic memory); diencephalic structures.

Executive Function

Purpose

Assess planning, sequencing, and set-shifting.

Technique

Luria hand sequence, trail-making, go/no-go tasks.

Abnormal Findings

Perseveration, difficulty shifting sets.

Localization

Frontal lobe / frontal-subcortical circuits.

Calculations

Purpose

Screen parietal (angular gyrus) function.

Technique

Serial 7s, simple arithmetic.

Abnormal Findings

Acalculia.

Localization

Dominant angular gyrus (part of Gerstmann syndrome).

Abstract Thinking

Purpose

Screen frontal lobe function.

Technique

Proverb interpretation, similarities ("how are an apple and orange alike?").

Abnormal Findings

Concrete interpretation.

Localization

Frontal lobe.

Fund of Knowledge

Purpose

Rough estimate of baseline cognitive/educational level for context.

Technique

Current events, presidents, general knowledge questions calibrated to the patient's background.

Abnormal Findings

Should be interpreted relative to educational/cultural background, not an absolute cutoff.

Higher Cortical Function

Purpose

Screen for apraxia, agnosia, and neglect — often missed if not specifically tested.

Technique

Praxis (pretend to use a comb/toothbrush), object recognition, line bisection/drawing for neglect.

Abnormal Findings

Apraxia, agnosia, hemispatial neglect.

Localization

Parietal lobe (usually non-dominant for neglect).

04 Cranial Nerves▸

CN I — Olfactory

Purpose

Assess sense of smell.

Technique

Test each nostril separately with a non-irritating scent (coffee, alcohol swab).

Normal Findings

Correctly identifies odor bilaterally.

Abnormal Findings

Anosmia, unilateral or bilateral.

Localization

Olfactory epithelium, nerve, bulb, or tract; orbitofrontal region if higher processing is affected.

What Structure Is Being Tested

Olfactory epithelium and CN I.

Clinical Pearl

Often skipped, but ask about smell after head trauma or with a suspected frontal lobe mass — unilateral anosmia can be an early sign.

Common Mistakes

Using an irritant like ammonia — that tests CN V (trigeminal), not CN I.

CN II — Optic

Purpose

Assess visual acuity, visual fields, and the afferent limb of the pupillary light reflex.

Technique

Visual acuity (Snellen), visual fields by confrontation, fundoscopy, pupillary light reflex.

Normal Findings

Intact acuity, full fields, normal disc, brisk pupillary response.

Abnormal Findings

Monocular vision loss (optic nerve), bitemporal hemianopia (chiasm), homonymous hemianopia (retrochiasmal), papilledema, optic disc pallor.

Localization

Depends on the pattern — retina/nerve, chiasm, or retrochiasmal pathway.

What Structure Is Being Tested

Retina, optic nerve, chiasm, optic tract.

Clinical Pearl

A relative afferent pupillary defect (Marcus Gunn pupil) indicates unilateral optic nerve/anterior visual pathway pathology.

Common Mistakes

Forgetting to test acuity and fields in each eye separately.

CN III, IV, VI — Oculomotor, Trochlear, Abducens

Purpose

Extraocular movements, eyelid elevation, pupillary constriction.

Technique

H-pattern eye movement testing, assess for ptosis, check pupil size/reactivity.

Normal Findings

Full smooth extraocular movements, no ptosis, equal reactive pupils.

Abnormal Findings

CN III palsy: down-and-out eye, ptosis, dilated pupil if pupil-involving. CN IV palsy: vertical diplopia worse looking down/in, head tilt away from the lesion. CN VI palsy: horizontal diplopia, failure of abduction.

Localization

A pupil-involving CN III palsy is compressive (e.g., PCOM aneurysm) until proven otherwise; pupil-sparing is more often ischemic/microvascular.

What Structure Is Being Tested

CN III/IV/VI nuclei, fascicles, or peripheral nerve.

Clinical Pearl

Always treat a new pupil-involving CN III palsy as a PCOM aneurysm until ruled out.

Common Mistakes

Not distinguishing pupil-involving from pupil-sparing CN III palsy — this distinction changes the urgency completely.

CN V — Trigeminal

Purpose

Facial sensation (three divisions), muscles of mastication, afferent limb of the corneal reflex.

Technique

Light touch/pinprick in V1/V2/V3 distributions, palpate masseter/temporalis while clenching, jaw jerk, corneal reflex.

Normal Findings

Symmetric sensation, strong jaw clench, normal corneal reflex.

Abnormal Findings

Sensory loss in a division (peripheral) or a crossed "onion-skin" pattern (brainstem — spinal trigeminal nucleus), weak jaw muscles, absent corneal reflex.

Localization

Peripheral nerve vs. brainstem (spinal trigeminal nucleus/tract).

What Structure Is Being Tested

Trigeminal nerve and its motor/sensory nuclei.

Clinical Pearl

An onion-skin sensory loss pattern on the face suggests a lesion in the spinal trigeminal nucleus/tract (lateral medulla).

Common Mistakes

Testing only the afferent (CN V) limb of the corneal reflex without separately confirming the efferent (CN VII) limb.

CN VII — Facial

Purpose

Facial muscle movement, taste to the anterior two-thirds of the tongue, efferent limb of the corneal reflex.

Technique

Raise eyebrows, close eyes tightly, smile, puff cheeks.

Normal Findings

Symmetric facial movement, including the forehead.

Abnormal Findings

UMN pattern (forehead spared, lower face weak) vs. LMN pattern (entire hemiface weak, including forehead — the Bell's palsy pattern).

Localization

UMN: contralateral cortex/corticobulbar tract. LMN: ipsilateral facial nerve or nucleus.

What Structure Is Being Tested

Facial nerve, facial nucleus, corticobulbar tract.

Clinical Pearl

Forehead sparing means UMN (contralateral cortex); forehead involvement means LMN (ipsilateral nerve) — because the forehead has bilateral cortical innervation.

Common Mistakes

Not testing forehead movement separately from the lower face, which is exactly what distinguishes UMN from LMN patterns.

CN VIII — Vestibulocochlear

Purpose

Hearing and balance/vestibular function.

Technique

Finger rub/whisper test, Weber and Rinne tests, assess for nystagmus/vertigo.

Normal Findings

Symmetric hearing, no nystagmus.

Abnormal Findings

Conductive vs. sensorineural hearing loss (Weber lateralizes to the affected ear in conductive loss, away from it in sensorineural loss); nystagmus/vertigo suggests vestibular involvement.

Localization

Cochlear vs. vestibular branch; peripheral vs. central vertigo.

What Structure Is Being Tested

Cochlear and vestibular nerve.

Clinical Pearl

Use the HINTS exam (Head Impulse, Nystagmus, Test of Skew) to distinguish benign peripheral vertigo from dangerous central vertigo in acute vestibular syndrome.

Common Mistakes

Not performing HINTS when it's actually indicated (acute, persistent vestibular syndrome).

CN IX, X — Glossopharyngeal, Vagus

Purpose

Palate elevation, gag reflex, voice quality, swallowing.

Technique

Have the patient say "ah" and observe palate elevation/uvula position, gag reflex, assess voice quality.

Normal Findings

Symmetric palate elevation, midline uvula, intact gag, normal voice.

Abnormal Findings

Uvula deviates away from the weak side, hoarse or nasal voice, dysphagia.

Localization

Nucleus ambiguus (medulla) or peripheral nerve.

What Structure Is Being Tested

CN IX/X nuclei and peripheral nerves.

Clinical Pearl

The uvula deviates away from the side of the lesion (the weak side fails to elevate).

Common Mistakes

Relying solely on the gag reflex, which is insensitive and nonspecific — a bedside swallow assessment is more clinically useful.

CN XI — Accessory

Purpose

Trapezius and sternocleidomastoid strength.

Technique

Shoulder shrug against resistance, head turn against resistance.

Normal Findings

Symmetric strength.

Abnormal Findings

Weak shoulder shrug or weak head turn.

Localization

Spinal accessory nerve; note the SCM's action is contralateral (the right SCM turns the head to the left).

What Structure Is Being Tested

Spinal accessory nerve.

Clinical Pearl

Because SCM action is contralateral, weakness turning the head to one side actually localizes to the SCM on the opposite side.

Common Mistakes

Forgetting the contralateral action of the SCM when interpreting a lateralizing finding.

CN XII — Hypoglossal

Purpose

Tongue movement and strength.

Technique

Tongue protrusion, observe for deviation, fasciculations, or atrophy.

Normal Findings

Midline protrusion, no fasciculations, no atrophy.

Abnormal Findings

Deviation, fasciculations (suggests a LMN process, e.g., ALS), atrophy.

Localization

Ipsilateral LMN lesion (nerve or nucleus) vs. contralateral UMN lesion.

What Structure Is Being Tested

Hypoglossal nerve and nucleus.

Clinical Pearl

In an LMN palsy, the tongue deviates toward the side of the lesion (the weak genioglossus can't push it away).

Common Mistakes

Confusing the direction of deviation between UMN and LMN patterns.

05 Motor Examination▸

Inspection

Purpose

Screen for atrophy, fasciculations, and abnormal postures before touching the patient.

Technique

Visual inspection of all four limbs and the face.

Localization

Atrophy + fasciculations suggest an LMN process; abnormal posturing suggests UMN or basal ganglia pathology.

Clinical Pearls

Fasciculations at rest suggest an anterior horn cell or peripheral nerve process (e.g., ALS).

Bulk

Purpose

Assess muscle mass.

Technique

Inspect and palpate major muscle groups bilaterally.

Localization

Atrophy suggests LMN pathology or disuse — focal (radiculopathy, mononeuropathy) or diffuse (myopathy, neuropathy).

Clinical Pearls

Always compare symmetric muscle groups side to side — mild atrophy is easy to miss without direct comparison.

Tone

Purpose

Assess resistance to passive movement.

Technique

Passively flex and extend the limbs at varying speeds with the patient relaxed.

Localization

Increased tone: spasticity (UMN, velocity-dependent, "clasp-knife") or rigidity (extrapyramidal, "lead-pipe" or "cogwheel"). Decreased tone: LMN, cerebellar, or acute UMN lesion (spinal shock).

Clinical Pearls

Cogwheeling is best elicited with simultaneous contralateral limb activation — have the patient tap their other hand while you test tone.

Power

Purpose

Quantify strength by muscle group.

Technique

Test against resistance, proximal and distal muscles in all four limbs.

Localization

A pyramidal pattern (arm extensors weaker than flexors, leg flexors weaker than extensors) suggests UMN; focal weakness in a myotomal or nerve distribution suggests LMN.

Clinical Pearls

Pyramidal-pattern weakness is classic for UMN lesions — know it well enough to recognize it at the bedside.

Pronator Drift

Purpose

Detect subtle UMN weakness not obvious on formal strength testing.

Technique

Arms outstretched, palms up, eyes closed — observe for pronation or downward drift.

Localization

Contralateral corticospinal tract.

Clinical Pearls

Drift with pronation is classic pyramidal weakness; upward drift without pronation can instead suggest a cerebellar or proprioceptive problem.

Finger Tapping

Purpose

Assess fine motor speed and bradykinesia.

Technique

Rapid thumb-to-index-finger tapping.

Localization

Slowed/decremented movement suggests basal ganglia (parkinsonism); clumsy/irregular movement suggests cerebellar or pyramidal pathology.

Clinical Pearls

Progressive decrement in amplitude with repetition is characteristic of parkinsonism.

Toe Tapping

Purpose

Same as finger tapping, applied to the lower extremity.

Technique

Rapid toe tapping against the examiner's hand or the floor.

Localization

Same principles as finger tapping.

07 Sensory Examination▸

Light Touch

Purpose

Assess large-fiber sensation.

Technique

Cotton wisp or fingertip touch, compare bilaterally.

Normal Findings

Symmetric perception.

Abnormal Findings

Reduced/absent — peripheral neuropathy or a central sensory pathway lesion depending on the pattern.

What Tract Is Being Tested

Primarily dorsal column-medial lemniscus, with some anterior spinothalamic (crude touch) contribution.

Pinprick

Purpose

Assess pain sensation (spinothalamic tract).

Technique

Sharp object, compare bilaterally and proximal-to-distal to look for a sensory level.

Abnormal Findings

Reduced/absent suggests a spinothalamic lesion or peripheral neuropathy; a clear sensory level suggests spinal cord pathology.

What Tract Is Being Tested

Lateral spinothalamic tract.

Temperature

Purpose

Assess the spinothalamic tract — often paired with pinprick since they travel together.

Technique

Cold or warm object applied to the skin.

What Tract Is Being Tested

Lateral spinothalamic tract, same pathway as pain.

Vibration

Purpose

Assess dorsal column (large fiber) function.

Technique

Tuning fork on bony prominences — great toe or ankle first, moving proximally if reduced.

Abnormal Findings

Reduced/absent suggests dorsal column pathology, peripheral neuropathy, or B12 deficiency.

What Tract Is Being Tested

Dorsal column-medial lemniscus.

Proprioception

Purpose

Assess joint position sense.

Technique

Move the distal joint (toe or finger) up or down with the patient's eyes closed, ask which direction.

Abnormal Findings

Sensory ataxia, positive Romberg.

What Tract Is Being Tested

Dorsal column-medial lemniscus.

Two-Point Discrimination

Purpose

Assess cortical sensory processing.

Technique

Calipers — find the minimal distance the patient can distinguish as two separate points.

Abnormal Findings

Impaired despite intact primary sensation suggests a cortical (parietal) lesion.

Graphesthesia

Purpose

Assess cortical sensory integration.

Technique

Trace a number or letter on the palm; the patient identifies it with eyes closed.

Abnormal Findings

Agraphesthesia, with intact primary sensation, suggests a contralateral parietal lobe lesion.

Stereognosis

Purpose

Assess tactile object recognition (cortical integration).

Technique

Place a common object in the patient's hand, eyes closed, ask them to identify it.

Abnormal Findings

Astereognosis suggests a contralateral parietal lobe lesion.

Extinction

Purpose

Screen for neglect/parietal dysfunction.

Technique

After confirming intact unilateral perception, touch both sides simultaneously and ask if felt on one or both sides.

Abnormal Findings

Extinguishing the affected side despite intact unilateral perception is a sign of contralateral (usually non-dominant) parietal lobe dysfunction.

Double Simultaneous Stimulation

Purpose

The formal technique underlying the extinction test — can be applied to sensory, visual, or auditory domains.

Technique

Stimulate both sides (or both fields/ears) at the same time and ask what was perceived.

Abnormal Findings

Extinction to double simultaneous stimulation localizes the same way as sensory extinction — contralateral parietal dysfunction.

08 Coordination▸

Finger-to-Nose

Purpose

Assess upper limb cerebellar function.

Technique

Patient touches their nose then the examiner's finger repeatedly; vary the target position.

Localization

Ipsilateral cerebellar hemisphere.

Clinical Pearls

Dysmetria or intention tremor that worsens as the finger approaches the target is a classic cerebellar sign.

Heel-to-Shin

Purpose

Assess lower limb cerebellar function.

Technique

Heel slides down the opposite shin from knee to ankle.

Localization

Ipsilateral cerebellar hemisphere — but also requires intact proprioception, so pair with Romberg to distinguish from sensory ataxia.

Rapid Alternating Movements

Purpose

Assess for dysdiadochokinesia.

Technique

Rapid pronation/supination of the hand on the thigh or opposite palm.

Localization

Ipsilateral cerebellar hemisphere.

Clinical Pearls

Irregular rhythm or rate is a classic cerebellar sign.

Finger Chase

Purpose

Dynamic cerebellar assessment.

Technique

Patient follows the examiner's moving finger with their own finger.

Localization

Cerebellar.

Rebound Phenomenon

Purpose

Assess cerebellar "check" — the ability to stop a movement.

Technique

Patient pushes against resistance; examiner suddenly releases and observes for overshoot.

Localization

Ipsilateral cerebellum.

Clinical Pearls

Positive rebound (overshoot, inability to check the movement) is a cerebellar sign.

Romberg

Purpose

Distinguish sensory (proprioceptive) ataxia from cerebellar ataxia.

Technique

Patient stands feet together, eyes open then closed; observe for increased sway or falling with eyes closed.

Localization

A positive Romberg (worse with eyes closed) localizes to the dorsal columns/proprioceptive pathway — not the cerebellum.

Clinical Pearls

"Positive Romberg" specifically means worse with eyes closed. Cerebellar ataxia is present with eyes open too — don't call cerebellar imbalance a positive Romberg.

09 Gait▸

Normal Gait

Description: smooth, symmetric stride, normal arm swing, narrow base.

Hemiparetic Gait

Description: circumduction of the affected leg, arm flexed/adducted with decreased swing.

Typical Causes: stroke, any unilateral UMN lesion.

Localization: contralateral corticospinal tract.

Pearl: the leg swings outward in a semicircle to clear the ground because of difficulty flexing the hip/knee.

Spastic Gait

Description: stiff-legged, scissoring of the legs.

Typical Causes: bilateral corticospinal tract lesions — cerebral palsy, cervical myelopathy.

Localization: bilateral UMN pathways.

Parkinsonian Gait

Description: shuffling, short steps, stooped posture, reduced arm swing, festination, difficulty initiating.

Typical Causes: Parkinson's disease, other parkinsonian syndromes.

Localization: basal ganglia/nigrostriatal pathway.

Cerebellar Gait

Description: wide-based, staggering, unable to walk heel-to-toe (tandem).

Typical Causes: cerebellar disease — alcohol, stroke, degenerative.

Localization: cerebellum, especially the midline/vermis for gait.

Pearl: the wide base persists whether eyes are open or closed — unlike sensory ataxia.

Sensory Ataxic Gait

Description: high-stepping, stomping gait; patient watches their feet while walking; worse in the dark or eyes closed.

Typical Causes: dorsal column pathology — B12 deficiency, tabes dorsalis, sensory neuropathy.

Localization: dorsal columns/peripheral proprioceptive fibers.

Pearl: dramatically worse with eyes closed or in the dark, since the patient is compensating visually.

Steppage Gait

Description: foot drop, high-stepping to clear the foot, slapping foot-strike.

Typical Causes: peroneal neuropathy, L5 radiculopathy, peripheral neuropathy (e.g., CMT).

Localization: peripheral nerve/root supplying the foot dorsiflexors.

Trendelenburg Gait

Description: pelvis drops toward the unaffected side during stance on the affected side — a hip "waddle."

Typical Causes: hip abductor weakness — superior gluteal nerve, L5 radiculopathy, hip pathology.

Localization: superior gluteal nerve/hip abductors.

Antalgic Gait

Description: shortened stance phase on the painful side to minimize weight-bearing time.

Typical Causes: pain — joint, bone, or soft tissue.

Localization: not neurologically localizing — a pain-avoidance pattern.

10 Examination in the Intubated, Sedated, or Comatose Patient▸

General Approach

Purpose

A systematic assessment when the standard exam (following commands) isn't possible.

Technique

Work stepwise: level of consciousness, brainstem reflexes, motor response, respiratory pattern. Minimize sedation first if it's safe to do so before over-interpreting the exam.

Pearl Always check for reversible causes — sedation, metabolic derangement, temperature — before treating exam findings as purely structural.

Level of Consciousness

Purpose

Establish depth of unresponsiveness.

Technique

Assess spontaneous eye opening, response to voice, then response to a central noxious stimulus (supraorbital pressure, sternal rub).

Abnormal Findings

Obtundation, stupor, coma.

Clinical Pearl

Use a central stimulus rather than nail bed pressure — nail bed pressure can be confounded by a spinal withdrawal reflex that doesn't reflect true arousal.

Pupils

Purpose

Assess midbrain function.

Technique

Direct and consensual light reflex, note size and symmetry.

Abnormal Findings

Unilateral fixed/dilated: uncal herniation, CN III compression. Bilateral fixed/dilated: severe midbrain injury, anticholinergic effect, or severe hypoxic-ischemic injury. Pinpoint: pontine lesion or opioid effect. Midposition and fixed: midbrain lesion.

Clinical Pearl

A new unilateral blown pupil in a comatose patient is an emergency — treat as uncal herniation until proven otherwise.

Corneal Reflex

Purpose

Assess pontine function (CN V afferent, CN VII efferent).

Technique

Light touch to the cornea with a cotton wisp or saline drop, observe bilateral blink.

Abnormal Findings

Absent reflex suggests pontine dysfunction or a CN V/VII lesion.

Clinical Pearl

Bilaterally absent corneal reflexes carry prognostic weight in the appropriate clinical context (e.g., post-cardiac arrest).

Blink-to-Threat

Purpose

Crude assessment of the visual pathway when formal visual field testing isn't possible.

Technique

Rapid hand movement toward the eye without touching it or creating an air current; observe for a blink.

Abnormal Findings

Absent blink can suggest cortical blindness or severe visual pathway injury — but this is a crude test, easily confounded by level of consciousness.

Oculocephalic Reflex ("Doll's Eyes")

Purpose

Assess brainstem (vestibulo-ocular) integrity in a comatose patient.

Technique

Turn the head briskly side to side only after the cervical spine has been cleared, and observe eye movement.

Normal Findings

Eyes move conjugately opposite the head turn ("doll's eyes present") — indicates intact brainstem pathways.

Abnormal Findings

No movement, eyes stay fixed with the head ("doll's eyes absent") — suggests brainstem dysfunction.

Clinical Pearl

Never perform this maneuver until the cervical spine has been cleared.

Oculovestibular Reflex (Cold Calorics)

Purpose

A more robust assessment of brainstem function when the oculocephalic reflex is equivocal or contraindicated.

Technique

Irrigate the ear canal with cold water after confirming an intact tympanic membrane; observe eye movement.

Normal Findings

In an awake patient with intact brainstem: nystagmus with the slow phase toward the irrigated ear, fast phase away ("COWS" — cold opposite, warm same, describing the fast phase). In a comatose patient with intact brainstem: tonic deviation toward the irrigated ear without the corrective fast phase, since that requires cortical input.

Abnormal Findings

No response at all suggests severe brainstem dysfunction.

Clinical Pearl

Don't expect nystagmus in a comatose patient — tonic deviation alone is the expected "normal" finding when the brainstem is intact but the cortex isn't correcting it.

Gag

Purpose

Assess CN IX/X function.

Technique

Stimulate the posterior pharynx, observe for a gag response.

Abnormal Findings

Absent gag — but note gag can be physiologically absent in many healthy people, so it's a less reliable sign in isolation.

Cough

Purpose

Assess brainstem/vagal function — often more clinically useful than gag for judging airway protection.

Technique

Observe cough response to suctioning or endotracheal tube stimulation.

Abnormal Findings

An absent cough correlates better with poor airway protection and extubation failure risk than an absent gag reflex.

Motor Response

Purpose

Assess the best motor response — an important localizing and prognostic element.

Technique

Apply a central noxious stimulus and observe the response.

Findings, Best to Worst

Localizes (purposeful reach toward the stimulus) → withdraws → abnormal flexion (decorticate) → abnormal extension (decerebrate) → no response.

Triple Flexion

Purpose

Distinguish a true motor response from a spinal reflex.

Technique

Apply a noxious stimulus to the foot/leg, observe hip/knee/ankle flexion.

Clinical Pearl

Triple flexion is a spinal withdrawal reflex, not a purposeful motor response — don't mistake it for volitional withdrawal. It can persist even with a complete spinal cord injury or in brain death.

Decorticate Posturing

Description

Arm flexion/adduction with wrist and finger flexion; leg extension.

Localization

A lesion above the red nucleus (cerebral hemispheres or diencephalon) — the corticospinal tract is disrupted but the rubrospinal tract remains intact.

Decerebrate Posturing

Description

Arm and leg extension with internal rotation.

Localization

A lesion at or below the red nucleus (midbrain to pons) — a lower, more severe lesion than decorticate posturing, and generally worse prognostically.

Pearl Decorticate (flexion) comes from a higher lesion; decerebrate (extension) comes from a lower, worse lesion. Progression from decorticate to decerebrate suggests worsening herniation.

Respiratory Patterns

  • Cheyne-Stokes: crescendo-decrescendo breathing with apnea — bilateral cerebral hemisphere dysfunction or early diencephalic involvement.
  • Central neurogenic hyperventilation: sustained rapid, deep breathing — midbrain/upper pons lesion.
  • Apneustic breathing: prolonged inspiratory pause — pontine lesion.
  • Ataxic (Biot's) breathing: irregular, unpredictable pattern — medullary lesion, often preterminal.
Pearl Respiratory pattern roughly tracks a rostral-to-caudal progression as herniation worsens — useful for following a deteriorating patient over time.

Brainstem Reflexes, as a Group

Pupillary, corneal, oculocephalic/oculovestibular, and gag/cough reflexes are used together as a "brainstem reflex battery" to assess overall brainstem integrity — this combined assessment is especially relevant in a brain death evaluation.

FOUR Score

Four components, each scored 0–4: Eye response, Motor response, Brainstem reflexes, Respiration.

11 Functional Neurological Disorder Examination▸

Hoover Sign

Purpose

Distinguish functional from organic leg weakness.

Technique

Place a hand under the heel of the "weak" leg; ask the patient to flex the contralateral (strong) hip against resistance; feel for involuntary downward pressure from the "weak" heel.

Interpretation

A positive sign — involuntary extension effort in the "weak" leg during contralateral hip flexion — supports functional weakness.

Physiology

Normal synergistic contralateral hip extension during hip flexion is an automatic, subconscious motor pattern. It persists in functional weakness but is typically also impaired in true organic weakness.

Clinical Pearl

Can also be tested dynamically: ask the patient to actively extend the "weak" hip (fails), then flex the good hip — the weak hip extends involuntarily.

Hip Abductor Sign

Purpose

Same principle as Hoover sign, applied to hip abduction.

Technique

Test hip abduction strength on the "weak" side while the contralateral leg abducts against resistance.

Interpretation

Involuntary abduction of the "weak" leg during contralateral effort supports functional weakness.

Give-Way Weakness

Purpose

Identify a non-organic pattern of weakness.

Technique

Test strength against resistance.

Interpretation

Sudden, inconsistent "giving way" rather than smooth, graded weakness suggests a functional origin.

Physiology

True organic weakness typically shows smooth, reproducible resistance up to a fixed limit; give-way weakness is jerky and inconsistent.

Clinical Pearl

Not specific on its own — pain, fatigue, or poor effort for other reasons can mimic it, so interpret in the full clinical context.

Collapsing Weakness

Purpose & Interpretation

Essentially the same phenomenon as give-way weakness — a rapid loss of initial resistance during strength testing.

Arm Drop Test

Purpose

Distinguish functional from organic weakness or unresponsiveness.

Technique

Lift the "paralyzed" arm above the patient's face and release it; observe where it falls.

Interpretation

In functional weakness/unresponsiveness, the arm typically deviates around the face rather than striking it; in true unconsciousness or organic paralysis, the arm falls directly onto the face.

Physiology

Reflects preserved subconscious self-protective avoidance in functional states.

Finger Abduction Sign

Purpose

Same concept as Hoover sign, applied to the upper extremity.

Technique

Test finger abduction strength on the "weak" hand while the patient performs a strong maneuver with the other hand.

Interpretation

Involuntary finger abduction on the "weak" side during contralateral effort supports functional weakness.

Midline Sensory Splitting

Purpose

Identify a non-anatomic sensory pattern.

Technique

Test sensation (vibration or pinprick) directly along the anatomic midline of the trunk, face, or skull.

Interpretation

An abrupt sensory cutoff exactly at the midline — rather than the few centimeters of overlap expected from bilateral innervation — is non-physiologic and suggests a functional sensory disturbance.

Tremor Entrainment

Purpose

Distinguish functional tremor from organic tremor.

Technique

Ask the patient to perform a rhythmic voluntary movement with the unaffected limb at a different frequency; observe the tremulous limb.

Interpretation

If the tremor entrains to the new rhythm, or stops/changes character during the voluntary task, this supports a functional tremor.

Physiology

Organic tremor (essential tremor, parkinsonian tremor) has a fixed intrinsic frequency that doesn't change with distraction; functional tremor is attention-driven and entrainable.

Distractibility

Purpose

A general principle across many FND signs — functional findings often improve or disappear with distraction and worsen with focused attention on the affected body part.

Technique

Engage the patient in a cognitively demanding task (serial 7s, conversation) while observing the affected limb or movement.

Interpretation

Improvement with distraction supports a functional etiology.

Astasia-Abasia

Purpose

Describes a functional gait pattern.

Technique

Observe gait.

Interpretation

Wildly fluctuating, elaborate, effortful-appearing gait with dramatic near-falls but rarely actual falls or injury — often improves with distraction and is incongruent with the rest of the exam.

Clinical Pearl

Patients often display remarkable balance during the dramatic swaying, avoiding actual injury — inconsistent with a truly severe organic ataxia.

Chair Test

Purpose

Assess for a functional gait/mobility disorder.

Technique

Observe the patient rising from a chair, turning, and sitting; compare the effort and pattern to formal strength testing findings.

Interpretation

Inconsistency between demonstrated functional capability (e.g., a normal chair transfer) and formal exam findings (claimed severe weakness) supports a functional diagnosis.

12 Neuro Tricks: Bedside Pearls from Experienced Neurologists▸

Check Sign

Purpose

Quick bedside screen for the cerebellum's ability to arrest a ballistic movement.

Technique

Have the patient forcefully flex the elbow against resistance; release suddenly and watch whether they can "check" the arm before it strikes their own body.

Why It Works

The cerebellum provides rapid feedback braking for fast movements — without it, the arm overshoots.

Localization

Ipsilateral cerebellum.

Clinical Pearl

Stand ready to protect the patient's face — overshoot can be dramatic in true cerebellar disease.

Orbit Sign

Pending — add your program's specific definition/technique for this one; not confidently verified from general knowledge.

Forearm Rolling Test

Purpose

Detect very subtle unilateral upper limb weakness — more sensitive than standard pronator drift.

Technique

Patient extends both forearms parallel with loose fists, then rolls one forearm around the other repeatedly, then reverses direction.

Why It Works

Subtle weakness causes the affected arm to become the relatively stationary axis while the normal arm does more of the rotating.

Localization

Contralateral corticospinal tract.

Clinical Pearl

Reach for this when pronator drift is equivocal but you still suspect subtle hemiparesis.

Finger Rolling Test

Purpose

Same concept as forearm rolling, using the index fingers.

Technique

Patient rotates their index fingers around each other.

Why It Works

The weaker finger tends to orbit around the stronger one, or shows reduced rotational excursion.

Localization

Contralateral corticospinal tract.

Barre Test

Purpose

Detect subtle arm weakness.

Technique

Patient holds both arms extended forward (or out to the sides), palms up, eyes closed; watch for downward drift or pronation.

Why It Works

Same principle as pronator drift — subtle weakness shows up as gravity-driven drift before it's obvious on formal strength testing.

Localization

Contralateral corticospinal tract.

Mingazzini Maneuver

Purpose

Lower extremity analog of the Barre test — useful in bedridden patients who can't stand.

Technique

Patient lies supine with hips and knees flexed to 90°, holding the position with eyes closed; watch for downward drift of the weak leg.

Localization

Contralateral corticospinal tract.

Clinical Pearl

Especially useful when a patient can't cooperate with a standing exam.

Pronator Drift Variants

Purpose

Increase the sensitivity of standard pronator drift for very subtle weakness.

Technique

Add a cognitive distraction task (count backward, answer questions) while the arms are outstretched, or briefly tap down on the wrists and watch the recovery.

Why It Works

Distraction reduces voluntary compensation, unmasking subtle drift that disappears when the patient is focused on holding still.

Clinical Pearl

Always test with eyes closed, and add a distraction task when you suspect very mild weakness.

Finger Escape Sign

Purpose

Detect subtle cervical myelopathy.

Technique

Patient holds fingers adducted and extended (a "high five" position); watch over 30–60 seconds for the ulnar two fingers to spontaneously abduct and flex.

Why It Works

Intrinsic hand muscle weakness from cervical cord compression preferentially affects the ulnar-innervated adductors, letting those fingers drift.

Localization

Cervical spinal cord, roughly the C8 level — myelopathy.

Clinical Pearl

A subtle but important early sign of cervical myelopathy — easy to miss if you're not specifically looking for it.

Upgoing Thumb Sign

Purpose

An upper extremity pyramidal sign, part of the Hoffmann reflex complex.

Technique

Flick the nail of the middle finger downward; watch for involuntary flexion/adduction of the thumb.

Localization

Corticospinal tract, at or above the cervical spinal cord.

Clinical Pearl

Think of it as the upper-extremity analog of Babinski — a pyramidal sign, not a lower motor neuron finding.

Subtle Neglect Tricks

Purpose

Unmask mild hemispatial neglect that isn't obvious on gross exam.

Technique

Line bisection tasks, clock drawing or figure copying (look for asymmetric omissions), and double simultaneous stimulation even when unilateral testing is normal.

Why It Works

Subtle neglect may only appear under divided attention or competing stimuli, not with isolated one-sided testing.

Localization

Non-dominant (usually right) parietal lobe.

Clinical Pearl

Always test extinction with double simultaneous stimulation, even if unilateral sensory testing looks completely normal.

Bedside Cortical Sensory Tricks

Purpose

Detect parietal lobe dysfunction using graphesthesia and stereognosis.

Technique

As in the Sensory Examination chapter — but always confirm primary sensation (light touch, pinprick) is intact first.

Clinical Pearl

Cortical sensory testing is only meaningful once primary sensation is confirmed intact — otherwise an "abnormal" result just reflects the primary sensory loss, not a cortical deficit.

Subtle Cerebellar Examination Pearls

Purpose

Unmask mild cerebellar dysfunction that a standard exam might miss.

Technique

Perform heel-to-shin with eyes closed to remove visual compensation, or use a moving target (finger chase) rather than a static one for finger-to-nose.

Clinical Pearl

Subtle dysmetria is often more apparent chasing a moving target than during a static finger-to-nose test.

Eye Movement Tricks

Purpose

Detect subtle internuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO) or other subtle gaze abnormalities.

Technique

Test rapid saccades side to side, not just smooth pursuit.

Why It Works

INO becomes much more apparent with fast saccades (the adducting eye lags, the abducting eye shows nystagmus) than with slow pursuit, which can look deceptively normal.

Localization

Medial longitudinal fasciculus, brainstem.

Clinical Pearl

Never screen for INO with smooth pursuit alone — always add saccades.

Brainstem Pearls

  • "One-and-a-half syndrome" (combined INO + horizontal gaze palsy) localizes to a pontine lesion affecting both the MLF and the PPRF/abducens nucleus.
  • Skew deviation suggests posterior fossa/brainstem or cerebellar pathology, especially paired with acute vertigo.
Pearl Acute vestibular syndrome + skew deviation + direction-changing or vertical nystagmus should raise strong suspicion for a central cause (posterior circulation stroke) rather than benign peripheral vertigo.
